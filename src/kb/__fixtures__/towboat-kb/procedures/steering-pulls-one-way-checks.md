title: Boat pulls to one side under load — how to split it
applies_to: twin-screw towboats with hydraulic steering and flanking rudders

# Boat swings to one side with the lever at zero

Two things cause it, both get worse with power (so a big tow shows what a small one didn't):
**rudders not really at zero**, or **one engine pushing harder than the other**. Plus the one that
fooled everybody on the Crowley: **a rudder that isn't following its stock.**

## In order
1. **Ask the pilot:** how much rudder is he carrying, and does one engine feel like it's loading
   differently? Does it swing with the slightest input one way and take real effort the other?
   (That second answer = a constant push, not an offset.)
2. **Tow makeup:** loads one side / empties the other, or faced up off center. Nothing on the boat is wrong then.
3. **Dock measurement, pumps on, pilothouse at zero:** steering tiller-to-stop both sides, ram rod
   extension both sides, flanking rudders centered, feedback ball joints for play (pumps off, tagged),
   rams for creep over a minute.
4. **Rudder speed both ways:** time hard-over to hard-over each direction, watch pressure. Slower one
   way = that side of the hydraulic circuit (relief/check valve, ram seal, restricted hose).
5. **Under load:** at full ahead with the pilot holding her straight, measure again and watch the
   rudder gauge for creep. A ram or valve leaking down under load won't show at the dock.
6. **Engines at full ahead:** port vs stbd rpm, load/boost readouts. Quick test: ease the stronger
   engine's throttle a hair — if she straightens, it's thrust.
7. **Watch the wash with the rudders turned.** Both wheels' wash should deflect the same. One going
   straight = that rudder is not turning in the water, no matter what the tiller is doing on deck.
8. **Mark the stock:** line across tiller hub, nut, and stock collar; swing hard over. Line broken =
   sheared key / loose hub (above deck). Line intact = rudder not following stock (diver).

## Reporting
Loss of a steering component that reduces maneuverability may fall under USCG marine casualty
reporting. Captain and port engineer need it the same day; the reporting call is theirs.
